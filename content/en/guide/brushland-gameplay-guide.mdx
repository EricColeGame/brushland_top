## Why Brushland's Painting-First Design Changes Everything

Most kingdom builders hand you a castle and ask where to drop it. Brushland asks you to paint the ground first — grass, stone, rivers, cliffs — and then waits to see what grows there. This Brushland gameplay guide breaks down every mechanic that has been officially confirmed, so you can plan a kingdom now and start painting the moment the demo arrives. If you have been looking for a straight answer on how this cozy builder actually plays, this Brushland gameplay guide covers the core loop, the composition strategies that matter, and the questions the developers have not answered yet.

Brushland is an upcoming cozy kingdom-builder from Transhuman Design, and its central idea is simple: you never place a building. There is no construction menu, no placement grid, and no prefab catalog. Instead, you paint the land itself — grass, rock, mountains, rivers, waterfalls, and roads — and the world responds. Houses appear where the terrain invites them, villages spread across the ground you shaped, and given enough room a small settlement can eventually bloom into fairytale castles.

That inversion matters because it moves the interesting decisions earlier. In a traditional city-builder, your attention goes to placement and adjacency bonuses. In Brushland, your attention goes to composition: where a river bends, how steep a hillside reads, whether a valley has room to breathe. The building is the reward. The landscape is the game.

### What "no fail state" actually means

According to the officially stated design, Brushland has no time limits, no prefab buildings, and no losing conditions. Nothing punishes you for painting slowly or wiping a region and starting over. That removes the pressure that usually defines the genre — no starving citizens, no raid timers, no score screen — and replaces it with a sandbox where the only metric is whether you like what you see.

Practically, this means early sessions work like sketching. Paint a coastline, erase it, try a mountain range instead. The cost of experimentation is time, not resources, so there is no reason to hoard your brush strokes.

### Adaptive buildings, briefly

Buildings generate and adjust to fit their surroundings rather than being stamped down by the player. The direction is confirmed; the fine details are not. Expect homes to hug slopes, settle near water, and orient toward roads. The design implication is blunt: terrain shape is your only real lever, so make that terrain readable from a distance.

## The Terrain Brush Reference: What Each Stroke Does

Every element you paint serves a compositional job. A good Brushland gameplay guide should tell you not just what a brush does, but when to reach for it and when to stop.

| Brush | What it creates | Best used for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grass | Open, buildable ground | Foundations, farmland, visual breathing room | Painting too small — villages need space |
| Rock | Rugged texture and low cliffs | Edges, elevation contrast, transition zones | Overuse that fragments your districts |
| Mountain | High elevation and natural walls | Framing the kingdom, dividing regions | Blocking your own future road network |
| River | Water channels across the map | Connective tissue and a focal line | Rivers that start nowhere look accidental |
| Waterfall | Vertical water drop | Landmarks and drama at elevation changes | Placing one where there is no height drop |
| Road | Paths linking areas | Organizing growth and guiding the eye | Dead ends and loops that lead nowhere |

Three habits separate a readable kingdom from a muddy one:

- **Paint big shapes first.** Block in your major masses before you touch detail. Small strokes applied early get buried.
- **Give rivers a source.** A river should begin somewhere — a mountain lake, a spring, a waterfall — and flow downhill to an edge of the map.
- **Limit yourself to one hero landmark.** A single waterfall or dramatic peak gives the eye somewhere to land. Five of them cancel each other out.

## Step-by-Step: Your First Kingdom in Five Passes

The build order below mirrors the officially presented flow: shape the land, add contrast, carve water, connect with roads, then step back and let the settlement respond. Think of it as five passes over the same canvas rather than five separate chores.

| Pass | Action | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Paint broad grassland | Large clear areas give future buildings room and keep later features legible |
| 2 | Add rock and mountain height | Elevation frames the village and creates districts without needing walls |
| 3 | Carve rivers and waterfalls | Water reads as connective tissue and provides striking landmarks |
| 4 | Lay roads between areas | Roads organize the map so the kingdom reads as one coherent place |
| 5 | Observe, then refine | Buildings adapt to slopes and water, so awkward terrain becomes obvious fast |

The fifth pass is the one new players skip. Resist the urge to keep painting; instead, zoom out, look at where the settlement is forming, and fix the terrain that produced a bad result. If houses are crowding a slope, flatten it. If a district feels cut off, add a road or a gentler grade. The rest of this Brushland gameplay guide assumes you are iterating this way, because the game rewards revision over one-shot perfection.

**Composition tip:** treat the kingdom as a painting before you treat it as a city. Open grass, a strong mountain backdrop, one winding river, and clear road connections will beat dense detail spread evenly across the map every time.
