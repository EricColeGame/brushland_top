## What Brushland Is — and What It Isn't

Brushland asks you to do something most city builders never do: put down the building menu and pick up a paintbrush. Instead of dropping houses onto a grid, you paint grass, rivers, and mountain ridges, then watch a village grow out of the landscape you made. That is exactly why a Brushland beginner guide matters — this game rewards composition rather than speed, and new players who treat it like a traditional builder tend to fight the tools. This Brushland beginner guide walks you through the entire opening loop: paint the land, shape a settlement, grow the village, and read what your residents do next.

Brushland is an upcoming cozy kingdom-builder from Transhuman Design. According to the official listing, the game is marked "Coming soon," with a demo targeted for Q1 2027 and the full release planned for Q2 2027 on Windows, macOS, Linux, and Steam Deck. You can follow the store page at [Brushland on Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/search/?term=Brushland) for updates as development continues.

Before your first brushstroke, close the expectation gap. Brushland is a sandbox about how a kingdom *looks and feels*, not a spreadsheet about how efficiently it runs.

| What you do in Brushland | What you don't do |
| --- | --- |
| Paint terrain with brush tools | Pick buildings from a fixed catalog |
| Let structures adapt to their surroundings | Place prefabs on a rigid grid |
| Compose a scene at your own pace | Race a timer or chase a score |
| Watch villagers arrive and settle in | Manage fail states or losing conditions |

If you arrive expecting production chains and zoning puzzles, the first hour will feel aimless. If you arrive expecting a watercolor painting that happens to grow houses, everything clicks.

## Step 1: Paint Your Starting Landscape

Terrain painting is the defining mechanic, and it's the first lesson any Brushland beginner guide needs to nail. Treat the map like a canvas: block in the broad shapes first, then add landmarks. Every terrain element has a structural job in your kingdom.

| Terrain element | Role in your kingdom | Beginner tip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grassland | Broad foundation and open space | Paint large areas first for breathing room |
| Rock | Boundaries, contrast, texture | Use it to frame future village sites |
| Mountains | Height, drama, natural walls | Place at map edges or as backdrops |
| Rivers | Flowing routes through the land | Draw downhill for natural-looking paths |
| Waterfalls | Visual landmarks at elevation changes | Add where rivers drop between heights |
| Roads | Connections between areas | Place last to link settled zones |

Here is the order that works for a first map:

1. **Lay down grassland.** Cover wide areas with grass as your base layer. Open ground tells the game where a settlement can eventually form and keeps the scene readable from a distance.
2. **Add rock and mountains.** Introduce rocky patches and mountain forms to create height contrast and natural borders. This is what stops your kingdom from looking like a flat green rectangle.
3. **Draw rivers and waterfalls.** Carve water routes across the landscape, letting them fall where the terrain steps down in elevation. Waterfalls double as visual anchors that draw the eye.
4. **Connect with roads.** Finish by placing roads that link the zones you expect to develop, giving the map structure before a single villager shows up.

### Paint in Layers, Not in One Pass

The most common early stumble is painting every terrain type at once. Resist it. A simple grass-and-river start with one mountain backdrop gives buildings room to adapt and keeps your first kingdom easy to read. Add complexity later, once you can see how the settlement responds.

## Step 2: Shape Space for a Settlement

Once the base landscape exists, arrange it into a coherent place for people to live. Buildings in Brushland form and adapt based on their surroundings rather than being chosen from a menu, which means terrain decisions are building decisions. This is the part of the Brushland beginner guide where planning pays off most.

| Settlement stage | What happens | Your focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Painted land | Terrain exists, no residents yet | Composition and open space |
| First settlement | Buildings begin forming | Terrain quality around the sites |
| Growing village | Structures adapt and multiply | Connections between areas |
| Kingdom | Castles and larger structures emerge | Overall visual harmony |

Three things drive that progression:

- **Open ground** is your settlement canvas. Keep it flexible so homes have room to form and expand.
- **Natural features** give the village character. A river bend or a rocky outcrop is what makes one kingdom look different from the next.
- **Connections** make the layout readable. Roads and water routes organize the map and tell the eye where to travel.

A useful test: squint at your map. If you can still tell where the village is, where the water runs, and where the high ground sits, your composition is working.

## Step 3: Grow the Village and Read Your Residents

Residents are an active part of the settlement system. As your painted landscape develops, villagers arrive, move into buildings, and take part in daily life. A populated settlement is the visible payoff of all that terrain work — and it's the feedback loop that teaches you what to paint next.

| Milestone | What to look for | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readable base map | Grass, water, and one mountain | Gives buildings a clear environment |
| Defined open area | A space where a settlement can form | Prevents cramped, unreadable layouts |
| First buildings | Structures adapting to terrain | Confirms your terrain choices work |
| Villagers move in | Residents occupying homes | Shows the loop is running |
| Road network | Developed zones linked together | Keeps the kingdom legible |
| Castle stage | Larger structures emerging | Signals kingdom-level growth |

One honest caveat that any good Brushland beginner guide should include: detailed professions, needs, and satisfaction systems have not been publicly detailed yet. Community reports and official material confirm only the basics — villagers arrive, move into buildings, and participate in daily life. Plan around what you can see, not around systems that haven't been shown.

Pace yourself. There are no time limits and no fail conditions, so growing slowly lets you watch how each terrain change ripples through the settlement. That observation is the actual skill.

## Common Beginner Mistakes and How to Fix Them

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Better approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Painting every terrain type at once | Cluttered map that's hard to read | Build in layers: grass first, then features |
| Ignoring elevation | Flat, lifeless landscape | Use height for waterfalls and framing |
| Skipping roads | Areas feel disconnected | Link zones as they develop |
| Rushing toward a big kingdom | Buildings adapt better to simple starts | Let a small village establish first |
| Treating it like a city builder | Wrong expectations, quick frustration | Embrace sandbox composition instead |

The thread running through all five is impatience. Brushland's brush system rewards restraint, and a plain map with one strong landmark almost always outperforms a busy one with six weak ones.

**Think like a painter.** Judge your kingdom by how it reads as a scene: contrast between open grass and rocky heights, water threading through the middle, warm buildings nestled between them. That composition mindset is the real beginner skill in Brushland, and it's the one thing no tooltip can teach you.
