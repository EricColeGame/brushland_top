## Brushland at a Glance: The Core Loop

Brushland is developed and published by Transhuman Design, the studio of Michał Marcinkowski — the creator behind Soldat and King Arthur's Gold. That background is worth knowing. This is a designer who builds systems that respond to player input rather than scripted campaigns, which means the game rewards experimentation over memorization.

The loop you'll repeat for hours is short:

1. Paint terrain (rock, water, grass, fields).
2. Paint buildings, roads, and farmland on top of it.
3. Watch the tiles resolve into something you didn't literally place.
4. Read the requests your villagers call out.
5. Paint the answer.
6. Watch the settlement grow into something bigger.

## Tile Adjacency Combos: The Real Engine of the Game

Here is the rule that a good Brushland gameplay walkthrough has to lead with: every tile looks at its neighbors and decides what to become. You are not placing a church. You are creating the conditions under which a house decides to become a church.

The developer's examples make this concrete. Ring a lone house with road and it resolves into a church with a belfry and a spire. Surround a house with fields and it turns into a windmill whose sails actually turn. Keep building density around your village core and the settlement eventually decides it has outgrown itself, sprouting a castle with round towers in pointed hats.

| What you paint | Where you paint it | What resolves |
|---|---|---|
| Road | Encircling an isolated house | Church with belfry and spire |
| Fields | Surrounding a house | Windmill with turning sails |
| Dense housing | Around the village center | Castle with round towers |
| Rock | Anywhere on the map | Mountains that catch snow |
| Water | Near a drop-off | Rivers that tip over as waterfalls |

### Why This Beats Blueprinting

Players coming from traditional city builders often try to plan a layout on paper first. That approach fights the game. Because resolution depends on neighbors, the productive habit is to paint in passes: rough shapes first, then rings and borders, then refinements. You're sculpting conditions, not drafting a floor plan.

The developer notes that even the person holding the brush can be surprised by the outcome. Treat that as a feature. If a house turns into a windmill when you wanted a bakery, you haven't lost anything — you've learned a rule.

## Painting Terrain: Mountains, Rivers, and Waterfalls

Terrain is where the simulation shows off most clearly. Lay down rock and mountains rise, tall enough to catch snow on their peaks. Lay down water and rivers find their own route downhill, spilling over cliff edges as waterfalls rather than following a path you drew.

That autonomy is the point. You suggest; the land decides. Two practical takeaways:

- **Work broad to narrow.** Block in your mountain ranges and coastlines before you touch individual tiles.
- **Let water surprise you.** If a river routes somewhere unexpected, that's usually a better composition than the one you had in mind.

There's also a safety net that makes terrain experiments cheap. According to the developer's description, the worst thing you can do is paint a lake underneath a crowd — the villagers cartwheel away shouting about falling, then swim off to find a new home. Even that is undoable. Nothing is permanently lost.

| Terrain brush | Typical result | Best practice |
|---|---|---|
| Rock | Snow-capped mountains | Paint ranges in broad strokes first |
| Water | Self-routing rivers and waterfalls | Follow the land's slope, don't fight it |
| Grass / open land | Buildable base for settlement | Leave room for road rings |
| Fields | Agricultural land around housing | Cluster around houses to trigger windmills |

## Villager Requests and Kingdom Growth

Once you place a dock against open sea, the first boats arrive. Villagers step ashore with suitcases, walk your roads to their front doors, and smoke starts rising from chimneys. From that point on, the settlement talks to you.

Requests surface as short callouts. One villager asks for a bridge. Another announces they want work. Your job is to answer with paint: a bridge across the water, a mine for the workers, a cottage with a view worth waking up for. Satisfy enough of them and word spreads that this is a happy place — more boats come, roads widen under the foot traffic, and the hamlet grows taller until it reads as a kingdom.

The idle chatter is worth listening to as diagnostic information, not just flavor. Villagers report what they're doing — fishing, milling, loitering on doorsteps — and occasionally haul a sheep around with no explanation whatsoever.

| Callout | What it signals | Suggested response |
|---|---|---|
| "Bridge, please!" | A route is blocked by water | Paint a bridge tile across the gap |
| "Work wanted!" | Population outgrew available jobs | Add a mine or production building |
| Idle chatter about milling | Windmill is functioning | Leave the surrounding fields intact |
| New boats arriving | Reputation is rising | Keep requests answered to sustain growth |

Community reports from the reveal coverage suggest the request system is the main driver of pacing — it's what replaces the clock. Answer requests and the kingdom expands; ignore them and it simply waits for you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there a full Brushland gameplay walkthrough available yet?

Not yet. The game is listed as coming soon, so no complete recorded playthrough exists. This Brushland gameplay walkthrough is built from the developer's published design details and covers the systems you'll interact with from your first minute.

### Can I fail or lose progress in Brushland?

No. There's no clock and no failure state. The harshest outcome described by the developer is accidentally drowning a group of villagers, who promptly swim off to resettle — and even that can be undone. Play boldly.

### How do I make a church or a windmill?

You don't place them directly. Ring a lone house with road tiles to produce a church with a belfry and spire, or surround a house with fields to produce a windmill with working sails. Adjacency is everything.

### Can I share the kingdom I build?

Yes. Finished builds can be exported as a short text code and sent to another player, and Steam Workshop support plus a level editor mean community-made content should extend the game well past launch.

## Final Tips for Your First Kingdom

Treat your first hour as a Brushland gameplay walkthrough of your own making. Paint a coastline, drop a dock, and watch who arrives. Answer the first few requests before you start decorating — growth is easier to style once it exists. Keep road rings tight around isolated houses so you get churches rather than sprawl, and cluster fields around housing to trigger windmills early.

Above all, resist the urge to plan. The pleasure here is watching tiles negotiate with their neighbors and produce something you only half intended. Paint broadly, listen to your villagers, and let the kingdom answer back.
