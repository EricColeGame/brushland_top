## Step 1: Paint Your Starting Landscape

Terrain painting is the defining mechanic and the biggest lever in any Brushland starting guide. Treat your first map like a canvas: block in the broad shapes, then add landmarks. Each element you can paint does a specific structural job, so it helps to know what you are asking each one to do before your brush touches the map.

| Terrain element | Job in your kingdom | Beginner move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grassland | Broad foundation and open space | Paint large areas first for breathing room |
| Rock | Boundaries, contrast, texture | Frame the sites you expect to settle |
| Mountains | Height, drama, natural walls | Push them to map edges or backdrops |
| Rivers | Flowing routes through the land | Draw them downhill for natural-looking paths |
| Waterfalls | Visual landmarks at elevation changes | Add where a river drops between heights |
| Roads | Connections between developed areas | Place last, once zones exist to link |

### The four-pass painting order

- **Pass 1 — Grass first.** Cover wide areas with open ground. Grass is your base layer, and generous patches keep the scene readable while leaving room for homes to form and spread.
- **Pass 2 — Rock and mountains.** Add rocky patches and mountain forms for height contrast. These frame future village sites and give the eye somewhere to rest.
- **Pass 3 — Rivers and waterfalls.** Carve water routes through the landscape, then drop in waterfalls wherever the terrain steps down in elevation. These become your map's signature landmarks.
- **Pass 4 — Roads last.** Once you know roughly where development will cluster, paint roads to link those zones. Roads painted first tend to lead nowhere.

The most common early mistake is trying to use every terrain type on the first map. A simple grass-and-river start with one mountain backdrop gives buildings room to adapt and keeps your first kingdom easy to read. Complexity is something you add later, once you can see how the settlement responds.

## Step 2: Shape Space for a Settlement

Once the base landscape exists, arrange it into a coherent place for people to live. In Brushland, buildings form and adapt based on their surroundings rather than being chosen from a menu, which means terrain decisions are building decisions. Leave a cluttered, jagged patch of ground and you get cluttered, jagged architecture.

| Settlement stage | What happens | Your focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Painted land | Terrain exists, no residents yet | Composition and open space |
| First settlement | Buildings begin forming | Terrain quality around each site |
| Growing village | Structures adapt and multiply | Connections between areas |
| Kingdom | Castles and larger structures emerge | Overall visual harmony |

Three things make a settlement site work:

- **Open ground** is your settlement canvas. Keep it flexible so homes can form and expand without crowding.
- **Natural features** supply character and framing. A river bend or a rocky shoulder gives a village an identity it would not otherwise have.
- **Connections** keep the layout legible. Roads and water routes organize the map and tell the player where one district ends and the next begins.

If a village site feels cramped, the fix is almost always upstream of the buildings — widen the grass, soften a rock edge, or reroute a river so it frames the site instead of cutting through it.

## Step 3: Grow the Village and Read Your Residents

Residents are an active part of the settlement system. As your painted landscape develops, villagers arrive, move into buildings, and take part in daily life. A populated settlement is the visible payoff of all that terrain work, and it is also your feedback loop — the way the village fills in tells you whether your terrain choices are working.

| Milestone | How you know you are there | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readable base | Grass, water, and one mountain in place | Proves the canvas reads clearly |
| Defined site | A clear open area framed by features | Gives buildings a place to form |
| First buildings | Structures appear and adapt | Confirms terrain is doing its job |
| Arriving villagers | Residents move into homes | Shows the settlement is alive |
| Linked zones | Roads connect developed areas | Turns scattered hamlets into a kingdom |
| Castle stage | Larger structures emerge | Signals you are ready to expand |

Pace yourself deliberately. Brushland has no time limits and no fail conditions, so growing slowly lets you watch how each terrain change ripples through the settlement. That observation loop is the fastest way to learn the system.

One caveat worth flagging: official material confirms residents arrive, move in, and participate in daily life, but deeper systems like professions, needs, and satisfaction have not been publicly detailed. Treat specific numbers you see floating around as player experience and community reports rather than confirmed mechanics.

## Common Beginner Mistakes and How to Fix Them

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Better approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Painting every terrain type at once | Cluttered map that is hard to read | Build in layers: grass, then features |
| Ignoring elevation | Flat, lifeless landscape | Use height for waterfalls and framing |
| Skipping roads | Areas feel disconnected | Link zones as they develop |
| Rushing toward big structures | Buildings adapt better to simple starts | Let small villages establish first |
| Playing it like a competitive city builder | Wrong expectations, quick frustration | Embrace sandbox composition |

The thread running through all five is the same: this Brushland starting guide is not teaching you to optimize, it is teaching you to see. Judge your kingdom by how it reads as a scene — the contrast between open grass and rocky heights, water threading through the middle, warm buildings nestled between. If a screenshot of your map would look good, you are already playing correctly.

When in doubt, make one change, then wait. Watch what the settlement does with it before you paint the next thing.
