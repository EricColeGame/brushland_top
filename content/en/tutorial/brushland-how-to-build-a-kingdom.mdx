## Brushland How to Build a Kingdom: The Six-Step Opening

The biggest mistake new players make is treating the brush like a decoration tool. In Brushland, terrain is the build menu. Every mountain you raise and every river you carve changes what can be built nearby, so the opening should be slow, deliberate, and geographic.

Think of your first session as a survey, not a construction project. Paint the shape of the land, leave yourself room to expand, and only then start placing homes. The table below is a reliable order of operations that works whether you want a coastal trading town or a mountain stronghold.

| Step | Action | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Paint a river mouth that opens into a calm stretch of coast | Give boats an obvious place to stop |
| 2 | Lay out a flat, dry core area a short walk back from the water | Reliable room for the first houses |
| 3 | Run a river or waterfall along the edge of that core | Water adjacency without slicing your buildable land in half |
| 4 | Raise a mountain ridge behind the settlement | A backdrop that influences house variants |
| 5 | Place a small cluster of homes and watch which variants appear | Learn the house rules before you scale up |
| 6 | Answer the first building request from the dock | Turn arrivals into permanent residents |

Steps 1 through 4 are reversible. Steps 5 and 6 are where commitment starts, because once a district fills in, repainting terrain around it becomes a demolition decision rather than a cosmetic one.

## Painting Terrain That Villagers Want to Live On

Community reports from early footage describe a land that reacts to composition rather than quantity. A single well-placed waterfall creates more identity than five scattered ones. A river that frames your town looks intentional; a river that cuts through the middle of it looks like an accident you will fight for the rest of the run.

Use the table below as a quick reference while you paint. It keeps the focus on what each terrain type contributes to a settlement instead of how pretty it looks in isolation.

| Terrain | Effect on your kingdom | Practical tip |
|---|---|---|
| Mountains | Strong vertical silhouette; nearby homes tend to change style | Place ridges behind districts, not through them |
| Rivers | Water access and a natural border | Use as a spine or an edge, never as a divider you cannot cross |
| Waterfalls | A dramatic focal point that defines a neighborhood | One per district is plenty |
| Flat ground | Reliable space for housing | Keep a reserve patch empty at all times |
| Coastline | The arrival point for boats | Keep the mouth clear of clutter and steep slopes |

A useful habit is to paint in passes. First pass: coastline and water flow. Second pass: elevation and mountains. Third pass: detail work like waterfalls and small terrain flourishes. Painting everything at once tends to produce a map that looks busy from above and plays badly from the ground.

## Housing Logic: Let the Neighborhood Decide the House

Houses in Brushland are not chosen from a menu — they respond to their surroundings. That single rule changes how you should lay out a kingdom. Instead of asking "which house do I want here," ask "what do I want this neighborhood to feel like," then place the terrain that produces it.

Player experience from the reveal suggests a simple principle: pick one dominant feature per district and commit to it. A waterfront row, a mountain terrace, and a waterfall overlook each read clearly on their own. Mix all three in one block and you get visual noise.

| Nearby feature | What to expect | Planning implication |
|---|---|---|
| Water | Homes that read as waterfront | Cluster along the bank for a coherent district |
| Mountain | Homes that respond to slope and stone | Build in a line that follows the ridge |
| Waterfall | Homes oriented toward the view | Leave open space in front of the falls |
| Mixed features | Blended, less defined variants | Split into two smaller districts instead |

There is a practical payoff beyond aesthetics. Districts with a clear identity are easier to extend later, because you already know which terrain to paint when you want more of the same. If you are ever stuck on Brushland how to build a kingdom that looks deliberate rather than random, this is the rule that fixes it fastest.

## Boats, Arrivals, and Building Requests

Boats bring villagers, and villagers bring demands. Every arrival is both an opportunity and a small test of your layout, because a newcomer who asks for a building you cannot place in a sensible spot will either wait or leave.

The key insight is that requests are directional. Boats come from the water, so growth follows the water. Keep your dock area flexible, keep flat land cleared nearby, and resist the urge to fill every open patch with houses the moment you can.

| Request type | How to prepare | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| A home | Keep flat, dry land cleared near the dock | Fastest way to convert an arrival into a resident |
| A civic building | Reserve a central plot in your core | Keeps the settlement readable as it grows |
| A production building | Place it near the terrain it depends on | Avoids reshuffling your town later |
| A repeat request | Expand in the direction the boats arrive from | Growth follows the shoreline instead of fighting it |

When a request does not fit your plan, it is usually better to repaint a small patch of terrain than to cram the building into a district it does not belong in. The brush is forgiving. A cramped town is not.

## Common Mistakes and a Repeatable Checklist

Most early kingdoms fail for the same handful of reasons, and none of them are about skill. They are about sequencing.

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Painting every terrain type at once | The map looks busy and districts blur together | Work in passes: water, elevation, detail |
| Building on the first flat patch you see | You lose the space you need for civic buildings | Keep a central reserve empty |
| Cutting the core in half with a river | Expansion becomes a bridge problem | Route water along an edge instead |
| Ignoring boat requests | Arrivals stall and the town stops growing | Treat requests as your priority list |
| Repainting around finished districts | Costs more effort than planning ahead | Sketch the whole map before placing homes |

A simple loop keeps you on track:

- **Survey first.** Decide where the coast, the river, and the mountain ridge go before placing anything.
- **Reserve the center.** Leave a clear plot for the buildings villagers will ask for.
- **Cluster by feature.** One dominant terrain feature per neighborhood.
- **Answer requests quickly.** Growth compounds when arrivals settle fast.
- **Expand along the water.** Boats are your growth engine, so build outward from the dock.

Community reports consistently describe this as a game where a ten-minute planning pause saves an hour of repainting. That matches the design: the brush is powerful, which means the cost of a bad decision is paid in terrain work rather than in currency.

## FAQ

**Do I need to plan the whole map before I start building?**
You do not need a finished blueprint, but you should decide where water, elevation, and your central reserve go. Those three decisions constrain everything else, and they are the hardest things to change once houses fill in.

**Why do all my houses look the same?**
Houses respond to what is nearby. If a district sits on uniform flat ground with no water, mountain, or waterfall in range, the variants have nothing to react to. Add one dominant feature and the neighborhood will start to differentiate.

**How do I get more villagers to arrive?**
Keep the water approach clear and answer building requests promptly. Boats bring newcomers, and newcomers who get what they asked for become residents — that is the core growth loop behind Brushland how to build a kingdom that actually expands.
