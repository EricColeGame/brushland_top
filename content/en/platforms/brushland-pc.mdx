## What Is Brushland on PC?

Paint a tile, and a kingdom answers. That is the entire pitch behind Brushland, Transhuman Design's fairytale kingdom-builder — and it explains why so many people are hunting for Brushland PC details long before the game has a release date. There is no download to grab today, no price to check, and no review score to trust. What does exist is a detailed store page, confirmed system requirements for Windows, macOS, and Linux, and a design philosophy that throws out timers and failure states entirely. This Brushland PC guide walks through everything confirmed so far and clearly flags what is still unknown.

Brushland is a city builder where you never place a building directly. Instead, you paint terrain, and each tile looks at its neighbors and resolves into something appropriate: rock becomes snow-capped mountains, water finds a path down cliffs as waterfalls, and a house ringed by road transforms into a church with a belfry and spire. The result sits somewhere between sketching and simulation — closer to Tiny Glade or Townscaper than to a spreadsheet-heavy management game.

The studio behind it is Transhuman Design, and the project is the work of Michał Marcinkowski, the creator of Soldat and King Arthur's Gold. That pedigree is worth noting. Those games were fast, physical, and chaotic; Brushland is deliberately slow, cozy, and gentle. What carries over is the systems-first instinct — building rules that surprise even the person who designed them.

## How the Painting and Building Systems Work

The heart of Brushland is adjacency. Every tile evaluates what sits next to it, and the world resolves accordingly. That single rule generates most of the game's charm — and most of its surprises, since the painter can be caught off guard by their own layout.

| What you paint | What appears |
| --- | --- |
| Rock | Mountains that rise and catch snow on their peaks |
| Water beside a drop | Rivers that find their own route down, tipping over cliffs as waterfalls |
| Road ringing a lone house | A church with a belfry and a spire |
| Fields surrounding a house | A windmill whose sails turn in the wind |
| Continued growth around the village | A castle with round towers in pointy hats |
| A dock on open sea | Boats arrive, unloading villagers with suitcases |

Villagers are the second layer. Once a dock exists, boats sail in and newcomers walk the roads to their front doors, with smoke rising from chimneys behind them. Ask what they are doing and you get small, funny answers — fishing, milling, dawdling on a doorstep. One is carrying a sheep and sees no need to explain. Later they start making requests: a bridge here, work wanted there, a cottage with a view worth waking up to.

There is no clock and no failure state. The worst outcome is painting a lake under a crowd, at which point the residents cartwheel away shouting for mercy and swim off to find a new home — and even that can be undone. A finished kingdom can also try on 42 color looks, then be exported as a short code to share.

## Tips for Your First Brushland Kingdom

When the game does arrive, the adjacency rules reward a little planning. These habits should pay off from the first session:

- **Lay one road spine first.** Roads are the connective tissue that turns scattered houses into a settlement, and a clear spine makes later growth easier to read.
- **Cluster houses before ringing them with roads.** A single house surrounded by road becomes a church, so save that pattern for when you actually want a landmark.
- **Reserve rings of fields for windmills.** Surrounding a house with farmland is the reliable route to turning sails and a working agricultural look.
- **Build the dock early.** Boats bring villagers, and villagers bring requests — the fastest way to unlock bridges, mines, and cottages.
- **Paint water last on slopes.** Rivers route themselves, so adding water after the terrain is set gives you better waterfalls.
- **Use the 42 color looks before sharing.** A palette pass costs nothing and makes an exported kingdom code far more striking as a gift.
- **Experiment without fear.** Nothing is timed and nothing is permanently lost, so the cheapest way to learn the tile rules is to break them on purpose.
