Brushland is one of the quietest overachievers in Magic: The Gathering — a two-color land that shows up untapped and asks for a small, recurring payment in life. Understanding Brushland features matters whether you're tuning a Selesnya aggro mana base, rebuilding a Premodern Enchantress list, or deciding whether a few-dollar rare deserves a slot in your binder.

The most important of all Brushland features is also the simplest: it taps for green or white mana immediately, with no "enters the battlefield tapped" clause attached. That one line separates it from most budget dual lands and explains why it has stayed playable for three decades. This guide breaks down the card's abilities, the deck-building tradeoffs those abilities create, and the practical buying details you need before you add a playset to your cart.

## What Is Brushland? A Quick Card Overview

Brushland is a rare land in green and white. It was originally printed in Ice Age and has reappeared in several core-set and supplemental printings since, including Fifth Edition, where the card's art was handled by Tom Wänerstrand. It belongs to a ten-card cycle players call the "pain lands" — a nickname earned from the life payment stapled to its colored mana ability.

Unlike dual lands with basic land types, Brushland carries no subtypes. It isn't a Forest or a Plains, so it can't be fetched by effects that search for those types. What it offers instead is unconditional speed: the mana is available the moment the land hits the battlefield.

## The Core Brushland Features That Shape Every Game

Three traits define how the card actually plays. Each one pushes deck builders in a slightly different direction.

### Untapped Mana on Demand

Brushland never asks you to wait. In a format where turn-one plays matter — a mana dork, a discard spell, a one-drop threat — a land that enters untapped is worth real percentage points. Community reports consistently describe pain lands as the cheapest reliable way to fix two colors without losing tempo.

### The One-Damage Rider

The colored ability reads: add green or white mana, and Brushland deals 1 damage to you. That damage happens as part of the mana ability itself, so opponents cannot respond to it, and it is damage rather than a life payment. Effects that prevent damage interact with it; effects that tax life payments do not.

### Colorless Mode as an Escape Hatch

You can always tap Brushland for a single colorless mana with no life loss. That mode matters in the late game, when you need one more mana for a colorless spell or an activated ability and would rather keep your life total intact.

| Ability | Cost | Output | Life change | Best used when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Colorless ability | Tap | One colorless mana | None | Casting colorless spells or activating generic costs |
| Colored ability | Tap | Green or white mana | −1 life | You need colored mana on curve |
| Land entry | Play as your land drop | Enters untapped | None | Any turn, no restrictions |

A useful rule of thumb from player experience: budget roughly one life per colored mana you actually spend. A deck that taps Brushland six times over a game is effectively paying six life for perfect mana — trivial against a slow opponent, meaningful against a burn deck.

## How Brushland Features Shape Deck Building

Because the card trades life for speed, it slots best into decks that plan to end the game before the life total becomes a liability. Aggressive Selesnya shells, Bant tempo decks, and Naya creature builds all appreciate an untapped green source on turn one. Midrange decks can run it too, but they usually want a mix with lands that cost nothing at all.

Control decks are the awkward fit. A control player often wants lands that enter tapped in exchange for card selection or extra value, and repeatedly shocking yourself to answer threats is a losing pattern.

## Buying Brushland: Printings, Condition, and Value

Brushland has been reprinted often enough that buyers have real choices. Older printings carry nostalgia and, in the case of the original Ice Age version, a distinct art style that some collectors prefer. Newer printings are usually easier to find in near-mint condition.

A snapshot of marketplace data from fall 2026 showed Fifth Edition copies starting around $3 for heavily played examples, with moderately played copies in the $4 range and near-mint listings closer to $7–$8. Supply was healthy, with dozens of sellers listing the card at any given time — a good sign that you don't need to overpay or rush.

| Condition | Typical market position | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Near Mint | Highest of the played tiers | Collectors and trade binders |
| Lightly Played | Slightly below near mint | Sleeved play, most buyers |

Three buying tips that save real money:

- **Compare printings, not just conditions.** A near-mint copy of a recent printing is often cheaper than a played copy of an older one.
- **Buy playsets when you need them.** Pain lands get sleeved together, and single-copy shipping adds up fast.
- **Check the official card database before trading.** The [Gatherer card database from Wizards of the Coast](https://gatherer.wizards.com/) lists current Oracle text, so you can confirm the wording on older printings still matches how the card functions today.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does Brushland do in Magic: The Gathering?**

Brushland is a land that taps for one colorless mana, or taps for green or white mana while dealing 1 damage to you. It enters untapped with no conditions, which is the core of its appeal.

**Are Brushland features good enough for competitive play?**

Yes, in the right shell. The card sees play in Modern, Legacy, and Premodern lists that need untapped two-color mana on a budget. It is weaker in slow control decks where repeated life loss adds up.

**Which formats is Brushland legal in?**

Brushland is legal in Modern, Legacy, Vintage, Commander, and Premodern. It is not legal in Standard or Pioneer, and it has never appeared at common, so it is not Pauper-legal.

**How many copies of Brushland should I play?**

Most decks run two to four copies. Treat it as a complement to fetch lands and shock lands rather than the entire mana base, and cut copies if your local metagame is heavy on aggressive red decks. The best way to judge is to track how often the life loss actually costs you a game — most players find it happens far less than they expect.
